## How we read the evidence

PubMed indexes 338 studies on nattokinase. Most of them cannot tell you whether it works in a person: 181 are in vitro and 94 are animal studies. Something that dissolves fibrin in a dish, or in a rat, may do nothing in a human being.

So this analysis rests only on **human evidence**: 11 clinical trials and 2 systematic reviews. Every one of them is listed at the foot of this page with its PMID. Where a trial found nothing, that is reported here in the same detail as the trials that found something.

<Finding>
The honest summary: nattokinase has a consistent, measurable effect on clotting factors and a modest effect on blood pressure. Its large effect on arterial plaque comes from studies of people who already had plaque. In healthy people with little plaque to remove, the best-controlled trial found no effect at all.
</Finding>

## What nattokinase is

**Nattokinase** is a fibrinolytic serine protease extracted from *natto*, a Japanese food made by fermenting soybeans with *Bacillus subtilis*.

A review of its mechanism reports that it hydrolyses fibrin directly, converts endogenous prourokinase to urokinase, degrades plasminogen activator inhibitor (PAI-1) and raises tissue plasminogen activator (t-PA) <Cite n="weng2017" />. That review does not compare its activity against pharmaceutical thrombolytics.

## Clotting factors: the most consistent finding

This is where the human evidence agrees with itself most closely.

<Study title="Kurosawa et al. 2015" design="Double-blind, placebo-controlled crossover · n=12 healthy men · single 2,000 FU dose" pmid="26109079" verdict="positive">
After one dose, D-dimer rose at 6 and 8 hours, fibrin degradation products rose at 4 hours, factor VIII activity fell at 4 and 6 hours, antithrombin rose, and aPTT lengthened.

The authors state plainly that **all changes remained within the normal range**. This is a measurable shift in fibrinolytic activity, not a drug-like anticoagulant effect.
</Study>

<Study title="Hsia et al. 2009" design="Open-label, self-controlled · n=45 (healthy, cardiovascular risk, and dialysis groups) · 4,000 FU/day for 2 months" pmid="19358933" verdict="positive">
Fibrinogen fell 7-10%, factor VII fell 7-14% and factor VIII fell 17-19%, with similar effects across all three groups. Blood lipids were unaffected. No notable adverse events.

Note the design: open-label and self-controlled, with no placebo group. That makes it weaker evidence than a randomised trial, and the effect size should be read with that in mind.
</Study>

## Blood pressure: modest and repeatable

<Study title="Li et al. 2023 (meta-analysis)" design="Systematic review and meta-analysis of 6 RCTs · n=546" pmid="39076715" verdict="positive">
Systolic blood pressure fell by a mean difference of **-3.45 mmHg** (95% CI: -4.37 to -2.18, p&lt;0.00001) and diastolic by **-2.32 mmHg** (95% CI: -2.72 to -1.92, p&lt;0.00001).

The abstract does not report the individual trial durations, nor the FU cut-off used to split its dose subgroups.
</Study>

<Study title="Kim et al. 2008" design="Randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled · n=86 (73 completed) · 2,000 FU/day for 8 weeks" pmid="18971533" verdict="positive">
In people with pre-hypertension or stage 1 hypertension, net change in systolic pressure was **-5.55 mmHg** (95% CI: -10.5 to -0.57, p&lt;0.05) and diastolic **-2.84 mmHg** (95% CI: -5.33 to -0.33, p&lt;0.05). Renin activity fell by -1.17 ng/mL/h.

This abstract reports no safety data, so no claim about tolerability is drawn from it.
</Study>

A few mmHg sounds small. In population terms it is not nothing, but it is also not a substitute for treating diagnosed hypertension.

## Arterial plaque: the strongest signal, and the strongest caveat

<Study title="Chen et al. 2022" design="Clinical study · n=1,062 · 10,800 FU/day vs 3,600 FU/day · 12 months" pmid="36072877" verdict="positive">
In participants with existing atherosclerosis and hyperlipidemia, the 10,800 FU/day group showed significant reduction in both carotid intima-media thickness and plaque size, with **improvement rates of 66.5-95.4%**. The lipid profile improved and no noticeable adverse effects were recorded.

The 3,600 FU/day group showed no effect on plaque or lipids. Subgroup sizes for the two doses are not reported, so the 1,062 figure is the whole cohort.

The authors also report apparent synergy with vitamin K2 and aspirin, and stronger effects in smokers, drinkers and higher-BMI participants. This was not a blinded randomised trial.
</Study>

<Study title="Ren et al. 2017" design="Randomised comparison vs simvastatin · n=82 (76 completed) · 6,000 FU/day · 26 weeks" pmid="28763875" verdict="positive">
Carotid plaque size fell **36.6%** with nattokinase against **11.5%** with simvastatin 20 mg/day. HDL rose in the nattokinase group and did not change on the statin; both lowered total cholesterol, the statin more.

CIMT of 1.13 → 1.01 mm (p&lt;0.01) is reported **pooled across both arms**, not for nattokinase alone.
</Study>

Now the trial that has to be read alongside those two.

<Study title="Hodis et al. 2021" design="Double-blind, placebo-controlled RCT · n=265 · 2,000 FU/day · median 3 years" pmid="33843667" verdict="null">
In people **without clinical cardiovascular disease** (median age 65.3), the annualised rate of change in carotid intima-media thickness and arterial stiffness did not differ between nattokinase and placebo.

The authors' conclusion: *"nattokinase supplementation has a null effect on subclinical atherosclerosis progression in healthy individuals at low risk for CVD."*

This is the longest nattokinase trial that exists, and methodologically the strongest.
</Study>

<Finding>
Two things separate Hodis from Chen and Ren, and both are plausible: the participants were healthy and low-risk, so there was little established plaque to break down; and the dose was 2,000 FU, well under the 6,000-10,800 FU used in the trials that saw plaque change. These are reasonable explanations, not proven ones. What can be said with confidence is narrower than the headline number: the plaque evidence comes from people who already had disease, at high doses, in trials that were not double-blinded.
</Finding>

Note that 2,000 FU is not an inert dose in general. Kim 2008 found blood pressure effects at exactly that dose <Cite n="kim2008" />. The threshold argument applies specifically to plaque.

## Cognition: no benefit to overall function

<Study title="Zhang et al. 2026 (ICC-PACS)" design="Single-centre, double-blind, placebo-controlled RCT · n=120 · 8,000 FU/day · 6 months" pmid="41325794" verdict="null">
In patients with asymptomatic intracranial or carotid stenosis, there was **no significant difference in global cognition** (MoCA mean difference 0.038; 95% CI: -1.109 to 1.163; p=0.948).

An exploratory analysis found a difference in visuospatial function (intergroup difference 0.350; 95% CI: 0.056 to 0.649; p=0.024). The authors describe this as suggesting "potential selective neuroprotective effects".

Exploratory findings in a trial whose primary outcome was null should be treated as a question for the next study, not an answer.
</Study>

## Cholesterol: nattokinase alone does not do this

This is where the supplement literature is easiest to misread, so it is worth being exact.

<Study title="Yang et al. 2009" design="Randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled · n=47 with hyperlipidemia · 6 months" pmid="19786378" verdict="caution">
Three arms: nattokinase alone, nattokinase combined with red yeast rice extract, and placebo.

**Nattokinase alone showed no effect on blood lipids** until month six. The combination improved every lipid measured from month one, with triglycerides down 15% and total cholesterol down 25%.

Red yeast rice contains monacolin K, which is chemically identical to lovastatin. The lipid effect in the combination arm is what you would expect from a statin.
</Study>

<Study title="Liu et al. 2023" design="Randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled · n=113 · 120 days · nattokinase-Monascus supplement" pmid="37836525" verdict="caution">
Significant improvements in total cholesterol, LDL-C and non-HDL-C in patients with dyslipidemia.

This is again a **combination product**: Monascus is red yeast rice. On the evidence of Yang 2009, the lipid effect is attributable to that component, not to nattokinase.
</Study>

<Study title="Li et al. 2023, lipid subgroups" design="Meta-analysis of 6 RCTs · n=546" pmid="39076715" verdict="caution">
In the lower-dose subgroup, total cholesterol rose (MD +5.27, 95% CI 3.74 to 6.81), HDL fell (MD -2.76, 95% CI -3.88 to -1.64) and LDL rose (MD +6.49, 95% CI 0.83 to 12.15, p=0.02). In the higher-dose subgroup, total cholesterol still rose slightly (MD +3.18) with no significant change in HDL or LDL.

The abstract states neither the units for these mean differences nor the FU cut-off dividing the subgroups.
</Study>

<Finding>
Do not take nattokinase expecting lower cholesterol. Every human trial that found a lipid benefit used it alongside red yeast rice, and the one trial that separated them found nattokinase alone did nothing. Hsia 2009 also found lipids unaffected.
</Finding>

## Thrombosis prevention

<Study title="Cesarone et al. 2003 (LONFLIT-FLITE)" design="Randomised controlled trial · n=204 · high-risk long-haul flight passengers" pmid="14565628" verdict="positive">
No DVT occurred in the treatment group. In the control group there were 5 DVTs (5.4%) and 2 superficial thromboses, 7 events among 92 people.

The product tested was Flite Tabs, 150 mg **pinokinase** — a combination formulation, not pure nattokinase. The result is encouraging for the product studied; attributing it wholly to nattokinase would go beyond what the trial shows.
</Study>

## Safety

Across every human trial identified, no serious adverse events were reported at doses up to 10,800 FU/day.

<Study title="Gallelli et al. 2021" design="Real-life recorded data · n=153 patients with vascular disease · 100 mg/day" pmid="34199189" verdict="positive">
No adverse drug reactions and no drug interactions were recorded during follow-up, in patients treated alongside fondaparinux, enoxaparin, or after vascular surgery.
</Study>

Chen 2022 recorded no noticeable adverse effects at 10,800 FU/day across 1,062 participants <Cite n="chen2022" />, and the Li 2023 meta-analysis reported no notable adverse events across its 6 trials <Cite n="li2023" />. Hsia 2009 reported none across 45 subjects <Cite n="hsia2009" />. Several other abstracts report no safety data at all, and are not counted here either way.

**Cautions.** Nattokinase measurably shifts fibrinolytic and coagulation markers <Cite n="kurosawa2015" /><Cite n="hsia2009" />. Anyone taking anticoagulants should discuss it with a doctor and monitor accordingly. Anyone allergic to fermented soy should avoid it. There is insufficient data in pregnancy and breastfeeding.

## What the evidence supports, and what it does not

| Outcome | Strength of human evidence |
|---|---|
| Lowers fibrinogen, factor VII and factor VIII | Consistent across trials, though the largest is open-label |
| Shifts fibrinolytic markers after a single dose | Double-blind crossover, changes within normal range |
| Lowers blood pressure by a few mmHg | Meta-analysis of 6 RCTs plus an individual RCT |
| Reduces existing arterial plaque at 6,000-10,800 FU | Large effects, but from non-blinded studies of people with disease |
| Prevents atherosclerosis in healthy people at 2,000 FU | No effect, in the longest and best-controlled trial |
| Improves global cognition | No effect in the only RCT to test it |
| Lowers cholesterol on its own | Not supported; effects come from red yeast rice combinations |

## Dosing, as far as the evidence goes

- **Blood pressure:** 2,000 FU/day was effective over 8 weeks <Cite n="kim2008" />. That trial did not run longer, so it says nothing about maintenance.
- **Existing plaque:** 6,000 FU/day over 26 weeks <Cite n="ren2017" /> and 10,800 FU/day over 12 months <Cite n="chen2022" /> are the doses where plaque change was measured. 3,600 FU/day produced nothing in the same cohort.
- **Above 10,800 FU/day:** not tested. No claim is made.

Nattokinase is not a treatment for diagnosed cardiovascular disease, and nothing here should replace medical advice.
